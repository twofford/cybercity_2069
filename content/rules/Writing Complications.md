# Writing Complications

How to come up with complications for failures, partial successes and Tension Tokens (see [[Rules Reference]]).

## The test

**A complication has to give the players a new decision, not a dead end.** "The bouncer says no" stops the scene. "The bouncer shoves you into a ganger who wants a fight" starts a new one.

## Where good complications come from

**1. Use parts that already exist.** The best complications add nothing new. They pull in people, machines and schedules that the scene already has: the bartender the PCs tipped, the building's one elevator, the 2 a.m. shift change. That's why they feel like the world reacting, not the GM making things up.
_Prep:_ give each situation four to six moving parts: people with wants, systems that work in specific ways, and things on a schedule.

**2. Someone wants something, and this gives them an opening.** Most complications are an NPC acting on what they want or fear. The informant sells the PCs out to cover his own debts. The receptionist wants something for her silence. A rival crew wants the same score.

**3. Machines break in ways that follow from how they work.** A retina scanner flags a cybereye's serial number. A smart lock logs every entry, including the PCs'. A drone's battery dies at the worst moment. Know how a system works and its failure is obvious, so every specific detail in your prep is a complication waiting to happen.

**4. Something on a schedule arrives early.** The guards change shift ahead of time. The shipment shows up an hour early. The police patrol loops back around.

**5. The PCs leave a trace.** A camera catches a face. A payment gets flagged. A fixer remembers who was asking questions. These don't hurt now. They come back later, and they show the players that their actions have consequences.

**6. Someone else shows up.** A news drone starts filming. Another crew is hitting the same target tonight. A street vendor saw everything and wants to be paid.

**7. Make it personal.** The guard at the door is a PC's cousin. The target's kid is home. Use it sparingly, because it has the strongest effect. A PC's Tie and Debt are built for this.

**8. The complication is an offer.** The corp security chief offers the PC a job instead of an arrest. A fence will pay double for something the PCs shouldn't sell. Temptation can be harder on players than punishment.

## At the table

When a roll fails, run through these in order until one sticks:

1. Who here wants something, and does this give them an opening?
2. What system is involved, and how does it break?
3. What's on a clock that could arrive now?
4. What trace did the PCs just leave?
5. Who else could walk in?

**For a partial success,** ask the same questions but keep the cost smaller: a price to pay, a delay, or being spotted, not a whole new problem.
